    Five things worth knowing

    Back to sleep, every sleep

    Placing your baby on their back, in their own safe space in your room, is one of the most protective things you can do for safe sleep.

    The six safe sleep steps

    Crying is usually highest in the first weeks, then eases

    Fussing and crying average about two hours a day through the first six weeks, then fall to about an hour a day by 10 to 12 weeks. It is not a sign you are doing something wrong.

    Conversation builds language

    Back-and-forth chats, where you talk, pause and answer your baby's sounds, are linked with faster language growth. In one study, 9-month-olds learned the sounds of a new language from a live person, but not from the same person on a screen or a recording.

    Allergy foods early, then often

    Giving egg and peanut soon after solids start, around 6 months, lowers the chance of allergy to them, and Australia's allergy experts advise keeping them in the diet every week.

    Your wellbeing matters too

    Up to 1 in 5 mothers, and up to 1 in 10 fathers, have anxiety or depression during pregnancy or after birth. It is common, treatable, and nothing to be ashamed of.

    Growing

    Milestones

    Milestones are the skills most babies show by a certain age: smiling, babbling, rolling, pointing. They arrive in a rough order, but every baby keeps their own timetable.

    What the checklists mean

    The checklists below come from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, revised in 2022 by paediatric experts. Each item is something at least 75 in 100 babies do by that age. That makes them a fair test of "most babies", so a missed item is worth mentioning to your nurse, but it is not a diagnosis.

    They were written for American children and adapted here into Australian English. Social and thinking milestones had the least research behind them, so treat those as the softest guide.

    For noticing, not testing. Babies often do things at home that they won't show at a check-up, and skills come and go before they settle. What matters most is the overall direction: steadily gaining new skills.

    Act early if you're worried. Talk to your maternal and child health (MCH) nurse or if your baby isn't doing one or more things on the list for their age, has lost a skill they used to have, or if something just doesn't feel right to you. Asking early is never a waste of anyone's time.

    The World Health Organization followed 816 healthy babies in Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States, and recorded when each reached six movement milestones. The bars show the age range in which 98 in 100 of them did so, from the 1st to the 99th .

    Newborn reflexes, and when they fade

    Babies are born with automatic movements called . As the brain matures, these fade and are replaced by movements your baby chooses. Nurses and doctors check them because the timing shows how the nervous system is developing.

    If your baby was born early

    For babies born before 37 weeks, use : their age since birth, minus the number of weeks they were born early. A baby born at 32 weeks is 8 weeks early, so at 4 months old (about 17 weeks) their corrected age is about 9 weeks. Use corrected age for growth and development until at least 2 to 3 years old.

    Babies born early reach reflex milestones on the same timetable as other babies once you go by corrected age. Vaccines are the exception: they usually follow the normal schedule from birth, not corrected age.

    How your baby's world sharpens

    At birth, vision is blurry and works best for big, high-contrast shapes, like a face close to theirs. Sharpness, focusing, sensitivity to contrast and the ability to use both eyes together all develop rapidly over the first months, as the parts of the brain that handle vision mature.

    Growing

    Play and curiosity

    Babies learn through people first. The most powerful thing for a curious, alert baby isn't a special toy or app: it's you, noticing what has caught their interest and answering it.

    Serve and return: the conversation before words

    When your baby looks, babbles, gestures or cries, they are "serving". When you respond with eye contact, words or a cuddle, you "return" it. This back-and-forth, called , shapes how the developing brain is wired.

    The way you talk matters. , the warm, slower, sing-song voice most people use naturally with babies, holds their attention. In a trial, coaching parents to use more parentese and more conversational turns led to faster language growth and better language at 18 months. Parentese spoken one-to-one, rather than in a group, was linked with more babbling and more words at age 2.

    Parentese uses real words, not baby talk. Stretch the vowels, ask questions, pause, and wait for your baby's reply.

    Reading your baby's cues

    "Let's play"

    Looking at you, reaching out, cooing, babbling, smiling. This is the moment to talk, sing or play.

    "I need a break"

    Turning the head away, arching the back, fussing or crying. Slow down, go quiet, or offer a cuddle. Pausing is a response too.

    Tummy time

    is awake, supervised time on the tummy. It strengthens the neck, shoulder and back muscles babies need to lift their head, roll and crawl, and it helps protect the shape of the head. Start from birth with a minute or two, a few times a day, when your baby is awake and alert, and build up. The Australian guideline is at least 30 minutes a day in total for babies who can't yet move about, spread through the day.

    A review of 16 studies linked tummy time with better movement skills and overall development, and with less flattening across the back of the head. Links with social and thinking skills were unclear, and no link was found with talking or fine hand skills. A flat area on the head, called , is common: in one Canadian study almost half of babies aged 7 to 12 weeks had some flattening, and most of it was mild.

    Play ideas for every stage

    These ideas come from the play tips that accompany the milestone checklists, and from studies of talking and reading with babies. Most are labelled "Weak" because simple everyday play is hard to test in trials, not because it might do harm.

    Books, from the very start

    You don't need to read the words. Point, name what you see, make the animal noises, and let your baby turn the thick pages. A review of 19 trials found that helping parents share picture books with their children improved how well the children talked and understood, and more sessions helped more. Those trials were in children aged 1 to 6, so applying them to babies is a reasonable extrapolation, not a direct finding.

    Moving, screens and the sun

    Floor time and freedom to moveSeveral times a day; not strapped in for long

    Australia's guideline for babies under 1 is to be physically active several times a day in many ways, especially through supervised, interactive play on the floor, and not to be kept in a pram, car seat or high chair for more than 1 hour at a time.

    ScreensNot recommended under 1 year

    Screen time is not recommended for babies under 1 year. In a large Japanese study, 1-year-olds who watched four or more hours of screens a day were more likely to have delays in communication and problem-solving at age 2. The study observed families rather than testing screens, so it can't prove the screens caused the delays.

    Baby walkersA well-known injury risk

    In the United States, an estimated 230,676 children under 15 months were treated in emergency departments for baby walker injuries between 1990 and 2014. Nine in ten injuries were to the head or neck, and most came from falling down stairs in the walker. Floor play and push toys used under supervision are safer ways to practise.

    SunShade, clothing and a hat

    Keep babies under 12 months out of direct sun when the UV Index is 3 or above, using shade, clothing that covers the skin, and a hat. Widespread use of sunscreen is not recommended for babies under 6 months.

    Baby massageA lovely time together; the claims go further than the evidence

    Many families enjoy massage as a calm, close time, and there is no reason to stop. But a review of 34 trials, most of them at high risk of bias (flaws in how they were run that can make results look better than they are), found that the apparent benefits for healthy babies, such as for growth, sleep and crying, rested on weak evidence. Where the authors could check, most benefits disappeared once the weaker trials were set aside; only one growth measure, length, held up. The authors concluded that the evidence doesn't currently support massage for healthy babies and their parents.

    Feeding

    Breastfeeding

    Breastfeeding is a skill you and your baby learn together. It often takes practice and support in the first weeks, and needing help is normal, not a sign that something is wrong with you or your milk.

    Why it matters, and what it doesn't do

    ½

    Any breastfeeding for at least 2 months was linked with about half the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). It didn't need to be breastfeeding only.

    2.5

    Let-downs in an average feed. Most aren't felt, and babies drink more when there are more of them.

    3×

    The range in how much healthy, breastfed babies drink in a day. There is no single "right" amount or schedule.

    Breastfeeding protects babies against infections and is linked with slightly higher scores on intelligence tests, and probably with less overweight and diabetes later. For mothers, it protects against breast cancer and may protect against ovarian cancer and type 2 diabetes. Many of these links come from studies that observe families, which can't fully separate breastfeeding from other differences between them.

    What the evidence doesn't show: the same large review found no clear link between breastfeeding and asthma or allergies.

    Australia's guideline is breastmilk only until around 6 months, then continuing to breastfeed while solid foods are introduced, until 12 months and beyond, for as long as you and your baby wish. Any amount of breastmilk counts. However you feed your baby, you deserve support, not judgement.

    Skin-to-skin, from the first hour

    Holding your baby naked against your bare chest straight after birth helps first feeds go well. In a review of 46 trials, it made it more likely that babies were still breastfeeding at 1 to 4 months. It keeps helping in the weeks after, too: when your baby is unsettled or a feed isn't working, skin-to-skin is a good place to start again.

    How milk is made and released

    Two hormones run breastfeeding. When your baby sucks, nerves in the nipple signal the pituitary gland at the base of your brain. It releases , which tells the milk-making cells, grouped in tiny sacs called alveoli, to make milk, and , which makes tiny muscle cells around them squeeze milk out: the .

    Because of this loop, how much milk you make depends on how much milk is removed. Because the breast makes milk to replace what is removed, feeding often, especially in the early weeks, builds your supply, and long gaps, or "topping up" without also feeding or expressing, can lower it.

    A deep latch

    , or attachment, is how your baby takes the breast into their mouth. An ultrasound study of 20 breastfed babies found that milk flowed as the back of the tongue dropped and suction peaked, which suggests suction does most of the work, rather than the baby squeezing or "milking" the nipple. A deep mouthful of breast lets your baby feed comfortably and well.

    1. Close, chest to chest

      Hold your baby close, their chest against yours and their nose opposite your nipple. Support the neck and shoulders rather than the back of the head, so their head can tilt back.

    2. Wait for a wide mouth

      Brush your baby's lips with the underside of your areola, the darker skin around the nipple. Wait until the mouth opens wide, with the tongue forward over the lower gum.

    3. Chin first, nipple to the roof of the mouth

      Bring your baby quickly to the breast, chin first, aiming your nipple at the roof of their mouth, so they take a big mouthful of breast from below the nipple.

    4. Check

      Mouth wide, chin pressed into your breast, nose clear or just touching, more of the areola taken in from below than above.

    5. If it hurts, start again

      Some tenderness in the first days is common, but pain that continues usually means the latch is shallow. Gently break the suction, take your baby off, and try again.

    Feeding positionsFive holds to try; comfort is the test

    There is no single right position. In every one, bring your baby in close, chest to chest.

    Cradle
    Your baby lies along the forearm on the same side as the breast you're using, head in the crook of your arm.
    Cross-cradle
    Your baby lies across your body along the opposite forearm, your hand supporting the neck and shoulders. Gives more control while you're both learning.
    Underarm
    Your baby is tucked at your side, under your arm, their back along your forearm. Often comfortable after a caesarean.
    Lying on your side
    You and your baby lie facing each other. Good for resting.
    Laid-back
    You lean back, supported by pillows, with your baby lying tummy-down on you. Can help if your milk flows fast.

    If you feed lying down in bed, read the co-sleeping safety advice in Safe sleep in case you fall asleep.

    Feeding cues: feed before the crying

    Early cues

    "I'm hungry"

    Stirring, opening the mouth, turning the head, rooting (turning towards a touch on the cheek, searching for the breast).

    Mid cues

    "I'm really hungry"

    Stretching, moving more, bringing a hand to the mouth.

    Late cues

    "Calm me, then feed me"

    Crying, agitated movements, turning red. Settle your baby first, with a cuddle or skin-to-skin, then feed.

    Cues from the Queensland Health feeding cues guide. In the first weeks, most babies feed 8 to 14 or more times in 24 hours, and many have , a few hours of very frequent feeds, often followed by a longer sleep.

    The first week, day by day

    At first your breasts make , a thick, yellowish early milk, in small amounts that suit a newborn's small stomach. Your milk usually "comes in" between days 2 and 5. Your baby's first poos are , black and sticky, and change to soft yellow by about day 5. Choose a day to see what's typical.

    Nearly all babies lose some weight after birth. In a study of more than 100,000 breastfed newborns, about 5 in 100 born vaginally and more than 10 in 100 born by caesarean had lost 10% or more of their birth weight by 48 hours. A baby who is feeding well is usually back to birth weight by 2 weeks, then gains on average at least 150 to 200 grams a week for the first three months. Your midwife or MCH nurse will weigh your baby and help if the numbers are off track.

    When things are hard

    Sore or damaged nipplesUsually a latch problem, and fixable

    Ongoing pain usually means your baby is feeding from the nipple rather than taking a deep mouthful of breast. Work on the latch steps above, and ask for hands-on help early: from your midwife, maternal and child health (MCH) nurse, a (a health professional who specialises in breastfeeding) or the Australian Breastfeeding Association.

    Very full, firm breastsEngorgement, often when milk comes in

    is breasts that feel very full, firm and sore, often between days 2 and 5. Breastfeeding medicine experts now see it as part of the same spectrum as mastitis, with swelling and inflammation rather than just "too much milk". Their advice:

    • Feed your baby as usual, when they're hungry. You don't need to "empty" the breast.
    • Avoid pumping extra milk "to clear it", which makes more milk and more swelling.
    • Use cold packs between feeds, and ask your pharmacist about anti-inflammatory pain relief that is safe while breastfeeding.
    • Skip deep massage and heat. Very light strokes from the breast towards the armpit can help move fluid.
    A painful, red, hot areaMastitis

    is inflammation in the breast: a painful, red or hot area, often with flu-like aches. Keep feeding as usual, follow the same steps as for engorgement, and rest. Antibiotics are needed only when a bacterial infection is likely.

    See a doctor if the pain, redness or firmness in your breast is increasing, or if a fever lasts more than 24 hours, especially with a fast heartbeat.

    "Am I making enough?"How to tell, and how to build supply

    The most reliable signs are your baby's nappies and weight. From day 5, look for at least 5 heavy wet disposable nappies (or 6 very wet cloth nappies) a day, with pale wee, and soft yellow poo. Then look for steady weight gain at your nurse visits.

    Frequent feeding, or expressing after feeds, is what builds supply. If you're worried, ask someone skilled to watch a whole feed. If your baby does need top-ups, they work best as part of a plan that also protects your supply, such as expressing when your baby has formula.

    Expressing and storing breastmilk

    Expressing means removing milk from the breast by hand or with a pump, so someone else can feed it to your baby later. Store it in a clean container, labelled with the date, and use the oldest first.

    Feeding

    Formula feeding

    Infant formula is the safe alternative to breastmilk for babies who aren't breastfed, or who have both. Making each bottle carefully, and feeding in response to your baby, matter more than the brand.

    Choosing a formula

    A standard cow's-milk-based "starter" formula, sometimes labelled stage 1, suits healthy babies from birth to 12 months. You don't need to move to a "follow-on" formula, and toddler milks aren't needed after 12 months.

    • Special formulas, such as those for reflux or allergy, are for use with medical advice.
    • Goat's-milk formula is not suitable for babies with cow's milk allergy, and soy formula isn't recommended under 6 months, or to prevent allergy. If your baby has an allergy, choose a formula with your doctor.
    • "Partially hydrolysed" or "HA" formulas, in which the milk protein is partly broken down, don't prevent allergy, so there's no need to choose one for that reason.
    • Changing brands often isn't recommended. Once a tin is open, use it within one month.
    • Cow's milk, goat's milk and plant milks aren't suitable as your baby's main drink before 12 months.

    Making a bottle, step by step

    Babies' immune systems are still developing, so a bottle needs a little more care than a cup of tea. Tick each step as you go; the list resets when you leave the page.

    1. Clean hands, clean bench

      Wash and dry your hands, and use a clean surface.

    2. Boil fresh tap water, then let it cool

      Boil fresh tap water and let it cool to lukewarm or room temperature. You can keep sealed bottles of cooled boiled water in the fridge for up to 24 hours.

    3. Water first

      Pour the correct amount of water into a sterilised bottle before adding any powder.

    4. Level scoops, from the tin's own scoop

      Use only the scoop in the tin. Fill it loosely and level it off: never heap or pack it. Follow the tin's instructions exactly, and never add extra powder or extra water.

    5. Seal, shake, check the temperature

      Put the teat and cap on and shake to mix. Drip a little on the inside of your wrist: it should feel warm, not hot.

    6. Feed straight away, then throw out the rest

      Make each bottle when it's needed and use it straight away. Throw out any formula left at the end of a feed.

    Wash bottles, teats and caps in hot soapy water after every feed, and sterilise them as the steriliser's instructions describe.

    Responsive bottle feeding

    Responsive feeding means following your baby's lead: feeding when they show hunger cues, and stopping when they've had enough, even if there's milk left.

    • Offer the bottle at early feeding cues, before crying starts.
    • Hold your baby close and fairly upright, and look at them as you feed.
    • Let your baby pause. Never push them to finish the bottle.
    • Most feeds take 20 to 30 minutes, and a feed shouldn't go on for more than an hour.

    Combining breast and bottle? Milk supply depends on how much milk is removed, so if you want to keep breastfeeding, express or feed at the breast regularly, especially when your baby has formula. Your MCH nurse or a lactation consultant can help you make a plan.

    Feeding

    Starting solids

    At around 6 months your baby is ready for more than milk. First foods are about iron, new tastes and textures, and giving the common allergy foods early and often.

    Is my baby ready?

    Start solids when your baby shows signs of readiness, usually around 6 months, and not before 4 months. Before then, breastmilk or formula meets all their needs.

    Steady head

    Good head and neck control: no longer floppy like a newborn.

    Sitting with support

    Sits upright with little or no support, for a short time.

    Interested in food

    Watches you eat, may reach for food, and opens their mouth when a spoon comes.

    Signs as described by Nutrition Australia. Milk stays important: keep breastfeeding or giving formula as solids build up over the months.

    Iron first

    is a mineral needed for healthy blood and brain development. The iron a baby is born with runs low by around 6 months, so the first foods should be rich in it: iron-fortified baby cereal, minced or finely chopped meat, chicken and fish, cooked legumes such as lentils, and tofu. Offer them in any order, alongside vegetables and fruit.

    Allergy foods: early, then often

    The advice on reversed in the past decade. Avoiding them doesn't prevent allergy; giving them early, and keeping them in the diet, does help.

    1 in 10

    More than 1 in 10 one-year-olds in a Melbourne study had a food allergy confirmed by a supervised food test. Egg was the most common.

    13.7% → 1.9%

    Peanut allergy at age 5 among babies at high risk: avoiding peanut, compared with eating it regularly from infancy.

    Weekly

    Once your baby has eaten an allergy food without a reaction, keep offering it at least once a week.

    A review of trials found moderate-certainty evidence that egg introduced at 4 to 6 months, and peanut at 4 to 11 months, lowered allergy to those foods. In an English trial, introducing six allergy foods from 3 months didn't lower allergy overall, partly because many families found the plan hard to follow; among those who stuck to it, allergy was lower, but that comparison is less reliable than the trial's main result, because families who kept to the plan may have differed from those who didn't.

    Australia's allergy experts recommend:

    • Give well-cooked egg and peanut soon after solids start, and the other common allergy foods (cow's milk, wheat, soy, sesame, tree nuts, fish and shellfish) during the first year.
    • Start with a small amount, such as a quarter teaspoon of smooth peanut butter or mashed hard-boiled egg mixed into food, and increase it next time.
    • Babies with eczema should still have allergy foods in the first year.
    • Never rub food on your baby's skin to "test" it.

    What doesn't prevent allergy: delaying allergy foods, avoiding them while you're breastfeeding, or choosing a partially hydrolysed ("HA") formula, where the milk protein is partly broken down, or a soy or other special formula. And the timing of foods containing gluten, such as wheat, doesn't change the risk of coeliac disease.

    Signs of a mild to moderate reaction

    Stop giving the food, stay with your baby, and get medical advice from your GP or a nurse line.

    • Swelling of the lips, face or eyes
    • Hives or welts on the skin
    • Vomiting
    • A change in behaviour, such as becoming very unsettled

    A mild rash only around the mouth, where food touched it, is usually not allergy.

    Signs of a severe reaction (anaphylaxis): call 000

    Hold your baby flat, not upright; don't stand them up or let them walk. If your baby has an adrenaline injector, give it now. Call 000. If in doubt, give the adrenaline. If your baby stops responding and isn't breathing normally, start CPR.

    • Difficult or noisy breathing, wheeze or a cough that won't stop
    • Swelling of the tongue, or tightness in the throat
    • A hoarse voice or cry
    • Pale and floppy

    can happen without any milder signs first.

    From smooth to family food

    1. Around 6 months

      Smooth, mashed and soft

      Start with iron-rich foods, mashed or puréed, and soft-cooked vegetables and fruit. A teaspoon or two is plenty at first.

    2. The months that follow

      Lumpier, and finger foods

      Move on to minced, chopped and lumpy foods, and soft pieces your baby can pick up. Learning to chew needs practice with texture.

    3. By 12 months

      Family foods

      Your baby eats most of what the family eats, cut to a safe size, without added salt or sugar.

    Spoon-feeding or baby-led?

    In a "baby-led" approach, babies feed themselves soft finger foods from the start rather than being spoon-fed purées. A New Zealand trial of 206 families found that, when parents were taught how to lower the risk of choking, baby-led babies didn't choke more often than spoon-fed babies. Choking was not rare in either group: about a third of babies choked at least once between 6 and 8 months. Baby-led feeding didn't change body mass index (weight for height) at 1 or 2 years, though parents reported less fussiness with food.

    Many families mix both. Whichever you choose, your baby should always be sitting upright and watched while eating.

    Gagging or choking?

    Gagging is noisy, and normal

    is a reflex that pushes food forward when it's too far back. Your baby may cough, splutter, go red and have watery eyes. Stay calm and give them a moment to bring the food forward. In the New Zealand trial, baby-led babies gagged more often than spoon-fed babies at 6 months, but less often at 8 months.

    Choking is often silent

    Choking means the airway is blocked. Your baby may be unable to cry, cough or breathe properly, and may go pale or blue. This needs first aid straight away.

    If your baby is coughing hard or crying, the cough is working: stay with them, let them keep coughing, and watch closely. If it doesn't clear, call 000. If your baby can't cough, cry or breathe properly, start these steps straight away.

    1. Call 000

      Or have someone call while you start first aid.

    2. Up to 5 back blows

      Lay your baby head-down along your forearm or across your lap, supporting the head and jaw. Give up to 5 sharp blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, checking after each one whether the blockage has cleared.

    3. Up to 5 chest thrusts

      If it hasn't cleared, turn your baby onto their back, head down, and give up to 5 chest thrusts with two fingers in the centre of the chest, checking after each one.

    4. Keep alternating

      Keep going with up to 5 back blows and up to 5 chest thrusts until help arrives. If your baby stops responding and isn't breathing normally, start CPR.

    Based on the Australian and New Zealand resuscitation guidelines. A baby first aid course gives you hands-on practice, and many are short and run locally.

    Foods and drinks to leave until later

    • Honey, until 12 months. It can carry bacteria that cause infant botulism, a rare but serious illness.
    • Whole or chopped nuts, which can block the airway. Smooth nut butters and pastes are the safe way to give nuts.
    • Hard, raw pieces such as raw apple or carrot. Grate them or cook them until soft.
    • Added salt and sugar. Babies don't need them, so season the family's food after taking your baby's portion out.
    • Cow's milk as a main drink, until 12 months. It's fine in cooking and in foods such as yoghurt and cheese.
    • Juice, soft drinks, cordial, tea and coffee. Under 12 months, drinks should be breastmilk, formula, and from around 6 months, cooled boiled water.

    Feeding

    Hydration

    Under 6 months, breastmilk or formula gives your baby all the fluid they need, even on hot days. Water comes later, in small sips from a cup.

    What your baby drinks, by age

    1. Birth to 6 months

      Breastmilk or formula only

      Even in hot weather, babies this age need only breastmilk or infant formula. Don't give water unless your doctor advises it. On hot days, or if your baby has a fever, offer extra feeds instead.

    2. From around 6 months

      Add sips of water from a cup

    Safe sleep

    A few simple steps, used for every sleep, day and night, greatly lower the risk of sudden unexpected death in infancy, called . They're easiest when they become habit from the first night.

    The six steps

    1. On their back

      Always put your baby to sleep on their back, never on the tummy or side.

    2. Head and face uncovered

      Nothing near the face: no loose bedding, pillows or soft toys. A safe sleeping bag keeps covers where they belong.

    3. Smoke-free, before and after birth

      Any smoking in pregnancy more than doubles the risk, and cutting down or quitting lowers it. Smoke around your baby after birth also raises the risk.

    4. A safe place, night and day

      A cot that meets the mandatory Australian Standard, with a firm, flat mattress that fits snugly, and nothing else in it: no pillows, bumpers, soft toys or loose bedding.

    5. Their own space, in your room

      Your baby sleeps in their own safe space, in the same room as an adult, for at least the first 6 months.

    6. Breastfeed if you can

      Breastfeeding lowers the risk. Any breastfeeding for 2 months or more was linked with about half the risk of .

    The six recommendations of Red Nose Australia, the national safe sleep authority, updated 27 October 2026. Offering a dummy for sleep is also linked with a lower risk: see Dummies.

    A safe cot, and what to keep out of it

    Wrapping and sleeping bags

    Wrapping (swaddling) can help young babies settle. If you wrap:

    • Use lightweight cotton or muslin, and wrap from below the neck.
    • Make it firm enough not to come loose, but loose enough for the chest to move and the hips to bend.
    • Stop wrapping as soon as your baby shows signs of starting to roll, or at 3 months, whichever comes first. Swaddle bags that hold the arms in aren't a safe alternative.

    In a combined analysis of four studies, the link between wrapping and sudden infant death depended on position: it was strongest when wrapped babies were put down on their tummy, weaker on their side, and weakest, though still present, on their back. Limited evidence suggested the risk grew as babies got older. That's why a wrapped baby always goes down on their back, and wrapping stops once rolling begins.

    A safe sleeping bag can be used from birth: a fitted neck and armholes, no hood, the right size, and not weighted. Arms stay free once wrapping stops, and never wrap a baby inside a sleeping bag.

    Too hot, too cold?

    There is no perfect room temperature, and you don't need a thermometer. Dress your baby as you would dress yourself to be comfortable. To check, feel your baby's back or tummy, which should feel warm. Cool hands and feet are normal; flushed cheeks or sweating mean it's time to remove a layer.

    The safest place for your baby to sleep is their own safe space. Red Nose doesn't recommend sharing a sleep surface, but many parents do fall asleep while feeding or settling, so it's worth planning for.

    If you do share a bed: put your baby on their back, beside one parent and never between two adults, away from the edge, other children and pets.     What the research says: in a large combined analysis, bed sharing raised the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) even when parents didn't smoke, drink or use drugs, especially for babies under 3 months, although the absolute risk stayed low. Another analysis, of two English studies, found no clear extra risk from bed sharing when none of those hazards was present, though it couldn't rule out a small risk for babies under 3 months. In the same studies, sharing a sofa, or sleeping next to a parent who had drunk more than about one and a half standard drinks, carried a very high risk at any age. So the risk of bed sharing without hazards remains debated; the hazards themselves are not.

    Sleep and crying

    Babies sleep a lot, but in short pieces, and waking at night is normal for most of the first year. Crying is how babies communicate, and in the early weeks there can be a lot of it.

    How much sleep?

    Day and night

    Babies aren't born with a working body clock, the internal rhythm that makes us sleepy at night. Within the first three months, most gradually start to organise sleeping and waking around daylight and darkness. You can help: keep days light and normal, with curtains a little open and everyday noise, and keep nights quiet and dim, with calm, brief night feeds. Keeping a newborn awake in the day won't make them sleep more at night.

    Waking at night is normal

    28–57%

    of babies at 6 and 12 months did not sleep 6 to 8 hours in a row, depending on the definition used.

    No link

    between not sleeping through and the babies' development, or their mothers' mood, in the same Canadian study.

    Babies under 6 months usually wake regularly and need night feeds. Sleep-training methods aren't recommended at this age, because there's no evidence they help; responding to your baby's cues is what matters.

    Tired signs

    Yawning, rubbing eyes, losing interest in people and play, fussing, and jerky arm or leg movements. Starting to settle at the first tired signs is often easier than waiting until your baby is overtired.

    Settling, by age

    Help your baby feel calm before sleep, and respond when they need you. Hands-on settling suits babies of all ages, and younger babies often respond best to it.

    • Feed, cuddle, rock, pat, sing, or gently "shush". Whatever settles your baby is fine.
    • Swaddling can help young babies settle, but stop wrapping at the first signs of rolling, or at 3 months.
    • If you use a dummy, offer it for every sleep; it's linked with a lower risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). Don't put it back in if it falls out once your baby is asleep.
    • Put your baby down on their back in their own cot, every time.

    From 6 months, if sleep is a problem for your family, gentle methods can help, as part of a plan that includes a calm, predictable bedtime routine such as a bath, a story and a song.

    Camping out
    (also called transition settling): stay beside the cot until your baby falls asleep, then move a little further away every few nights. Gentle and gradual.
    Controlled comforting
    : comfort briefly, leave, and come back at gradually longer intervals. For babies over 6 months only.

    In a Melbourne trial of controlled crying, a method like controlled comforting, for babies aged 6 to 12 months with severe sleep problems, more sleep problems had resolved two months later, and depression scores improved, especially for mothers with high scores to begin with. By four months, the groups were similar. A later, larger Melbourne trial of controlled comforting and camping out followed the children to age 6, and found no lasting harms and no lasting benefits: no differences in emotional health, behaviour, stress, closeness with parents or attachment, or in parents' mental health. A smaller Adelaide trial found babies fell asleep faster and woke less, without higher stress hormones.

    Using these methods is a choice, not a requirement. Responding to your baby at night is equally valid if it works for your family.

    How much crying is normal?

    A review combining diaries from 8,690 babies in several countries found that fussing and crying average about two hours a day through the first six weeks, then ease. There was no universal peak at 6 weeks, as is often said: it was more of a plateau, then a fall.

    About 1 in 5 newborns cry a lot or are hard to settle. Crying is often worst in the late afternoon and evening, and usually eases after 4 months.

    Dummies

    Dummies are neither good nor bad. At sleep time they're linked with a lower risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS); used all day, for years, they bring more ear infections and bite problems. A few simple rules get the best of both.

    The balance

    What the evidence supports
    • Lower risk of SIDS. Across seven studies, babies who used a dummy for their last sleep had substantially lower odds of . Red Nose and the American Academy of Pediatrics both suggest offering one for sleep.
    • No harm to breastfeeding, in trials. In two trials of healthy, full-term breastfed babies, offering a dummy didn't change how many were still breastfeeding at 4 months.
    What to watch
    • Ear infections. In a Finnish trial, advising parents to use the dummy only for falling asleep cut all-day use by 21% and ear infections by 29%.
    • Teeth and bite. Dummy and thumb sucking are both linked with bite problems, and longer habits carry more risk. Dummies were linked with crossbite (where the upper and lower teeth don't line up) in baby teeth more than thumb-sucking was.

    Note: studies of dummies and SIDS observe families rather than test dummies directly, so they show a link, not proof of cause. The breastfeeding trials included only healthy, full-term babies whose mothers were already motivated to breastfeed.

    Using a dummy safely

    • If you're breastfeeding, Red Nose suggests waiting until feeding is going well, usually at 4 to 6 weeks. Bottle-fed babies can have one from birth.
    • Offer it for every sleep, day and night, but never force it. If it falls out once your baby is asleep, you don't need to put it back.
    • Never coat a dummy in honey, jam or anything sweet.
    • Keep it for sleep and settling rather than all day, especially if your baby gets ear infections.

      Limiting the dummy to falling asleep reduces ear infections.

        Tongue-tie: what the evidence says

        What it is

        The frenulum is short, tight or thick, or joined close to the tip of the tongue, so the tongue can't lift or reach forward well. When your baby pokes their tongue out, the tip may look notched or heart-shaped.

        Signs it may be affecting feeding

        Nipples that look flattened, creased or striped after feeds, or are cracked; pain during feeds; a baby who doesn't seem to get enough milk, or gains weight slowly. Many of these have other causes, so they need checking by someone skilled.

        How it's diagnosed

        Not from how the tongue looks. Australian dental guidance says it should be judged by how the tongue works, with a full feeding assessment, such as a lactation consultant watching a breastfeed. Help with positioning and attachment is often the first step.

        Releasing it

        A is a quick snip of the frenulum. In a review of five small trials, it eased mothers' nipple pain in the short term, but didn't consistently improve how babies fed, and no serious complications were reported.

        Lip-ties and “posterior” tongue-ties

        There isn't enough evidence that releasing a lip tie or cheek tie helps breastfeeding, and Australian dental guidance says “posterior tongue-tie” shouldn't be used as a diagnosis.

        Questions worth asking before any procedure: Has someone watched a whole feed? What else could be causing the problem, and has it been tried? What exactly will be released? What help will we have with feeding afterwards?

        Changing the plan isn't failing. If breastfeeding doesn't work out the way you hoped, grief, guilt and anger are common, and they deserve support too. In a large English study, mothers who had planned to breastfeed but weren't able to had the highest risk of postnatal depression, and the researchers called for compassionate support for them. In a UK survey, stopping because of pain or physical difficulty was linked with more symptoms of depression. Expressing, combining breast and bottle, and formula feeding are all ways of feeding your baby well.

        Your mental health

        Very common

        The baby blues

        Up to 80 in 100 women feel teary, up and down, or overwhelmed between about day 3 and day 10. It passes within days, and is different from postnatal depression.

        Common, and treatable

        Depression and anxiety

        Up to 1 in 5 mothers, and up to 1 in 10 fathers, have anxiety or depression in pregnancy or after birth. In studies using clinical interviews, up to 19 in 100 women had a depressive episode in the first 3 months after birth.

        Rare, and an emergency

        Postpartum psychosis

        Affects about 1 to 2 in every 1,000 mothers, usually starting in the first days or weeks. It needs urgent help, and the chances of full recovery are good.

        When to talk to someone: if you feel sad, have lost interest or enjoyment in things, or worry to the point of distress, and it lasts more than two weeks, comes more often, or affects your daily life or enjoying your baby. Your MCH nurse or GP may ask you to fill in the , a short questionnaire about how you've been feeling. It helps them see who might need more support; it isn't a test you can fail.

        Get help now if you, or someone you're close to, has sudden confusion, strange beliefs, hears or sees things that aren't there, or has big mood swings after birth, or if you're thinking about ending your life, or feel you might act on thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. Call 000 or go to your nearest emergency department. Lifeline is on 13 11 14, 24 hours.

        Counselling and talk therapy

        Talking therapy means meeting regularly with a trained professional, in person, by phone or by video, to talk through what's happening and learn ways to feel better. It isn't only for a crisis. Many mothers use it to get through a hard patch, and the trials below show it helps.

        Treats
        Depression after birth

        Across 43 trials with 6,270 people, psychological therapy helped depression in pregnancy and after birth, and the benefit lasted at least 6 to 12 months. It also eased anxiety and parenting stress.

        Prevents
        Depression in women at higher risk

        In 17 trials with 3,094 people, mostly women at higher risk, those who had counselling were about 40% less likely to develop depression than those who didn't.

        Connects
        Talking with other mothers

        In a Canadian trial of 701 mothers at high risk, phone support from trained volunteer mothers who had recovered from postnatal depression meant 14 in 100 had high depression scores at 12 weeks, compared with 25 in 100 without it.

        Kinds of help

        Cognitive behavioural therapy: learning to notice the thoughts and habits that keep you stuck, and practising new ones. Australia's national guideline recommends CBT or IPT for mild to moderate depression after birth.
        Interpersonal therapy: focuses on your relationships, your support, and the big change in your role.
        Counselling and guided self-help
        For milder symptoms, counselling, or a structured self-help program with a professional guiding you, may be enough.
        Help with you and your baby
        If depression makes it hard to connect with your baby, therapy for the two of you together can help.
        Talking with someone who has been there, such as Birth Trauma Australia's peer support or a parent group.
        Medicines
        For moderate to severe depression or anxiety, the guideline's first choice is an , a type of antidepressant. It isn't usually necessary to stop antidepressants while breastfeeding; talk it through with your doctor.
        Hospital care
        For serious illness, specialist mother-baby units let you bring your baby into hospital with you while you're treated.

        How to start

        1. Tell someone how you're really feeling

          Your partner, a friend, your MCH nurse. Saying it out loud is often the hardest step.

        2. Book a longer appointment with your GP

          Ask about a Mental Health Treatment Plan. It lets you claim Medicare rebates for up to 10 individual sessions a year with a psychologist or other mental health professional: up to 6 to start, then more after a review. The referral needs to come from your usual GP or practice, and sessions can be by video or phone.

        3. Ask about free perinatal specialists

          The Gidget Foundation offers 10 free sessions a year with perinatal mental health clinicians, in person at Gidget Houses or by video, with a GP referral and Mental Health Treatment Plan.

        4. Not sure where to start? Call

          PANDA on 1300 726 306 (7 days), ForWhen on 1300 24 23 22 (weekdays) to be guided to local perinatal services, or Medicare Mental Health on 1800 595 212 (weekdays). None of these is a crisis line.

        Words that can help you start

        • “I haven't felt like myself since my baby was born.”
        • “I'm finding it hard to cope, and I'd like some help.”
        • “I'm having thoughts that frighten me.”
        • “Feeding has been really hard, and it's affecting how I feel.”

        Worried about being judged? Many mothers hold back because they fear being seen as a bad mother. The people listed here talk with struggling parents every day, and their aim is to help you and your baby, together. Getting help is part of looking after your baby.

        A plan for the hard days

        When it all feels like too much, it helps to have a plan you made on a calmer day.

        1. Put your baby down somewhere safe

          On their back in the cot is safe, even if they're crying.

        2. Step away and breathe

          Take a few minutes in another room. Slow breaths out help your body settle.

        3. Call someone

          Your partner, a friend or family member, the MCH Line on 13 22 29 (24 hours), or PANDA on 1300 726 306.

        4. Do one small thing for your body

          A glass of water, something to eat, a shower, or a few minutes outside.

        5. Tell your GP or MCH nurse

          At the next chance, or today if this keeps happening.

        If you feel you might hurt yourself or your baby, put your baby somewhere safe and call 000 or Lifeline on 13 11 14.

        For partners and support people

        • Notice, and ask. If she seems low, anxious or not herself for more than two weeks, say gently what you've noticed, and offer to go with her to the GP.
        • Take things off her plate. Nights, meals, washing, and managing visitors.
        • Look after yourself too. Up to 1 in 10 new fathers has anxiety or depression. MensLine Australia is on 1300 78 99 78, 24 hours, and SMS4dads sends free texts to new dads with tips and check-ins.

        Your body after birth

        Your pelvic floorGentle exercises from the first days

        The is the hammock of muscles at the base of your pelvis that supports your bladder, uterus and bowel. Pregnancy and birth stretch it. Gentle pelvic floor exercises can usually restart about 48 hours after birth, even after a tear or stitches, but check with your doctor or midwife first. Function often recovers within the first year.

        Leaking urine, a feeling of heaviness or dragging, or pain are worth raising with your GP or a women's health physiotherapist. The National Continence Helpline is 1800 33 00 66.

        Getting moving againStart gently, build gradually

        Being active after birth is safe and helpful for women without complications. Australian guidelines encourage a gradual return to the usual adult activity guidelines, pelvic floor exercises, and making decisions about exercise together with your health professionals.

        In the first weeks, think walking, gentle tummy bracing and pelvic floor exercises. Have a pelvic floor and tummy check before running, high-impact exercise or abdominal programs, and aim to return to high-impact exercise steadily over 3 to 4 months.

        is a gap between the two halves of the front tummy muscle. In a Norwegian study of first-time mothers, 60 in 100 had a gap at 6 weeks after birth, 45 in 100 at 6 months, and 33 in 100 at 12 months. Women with and without a gap had similar rates of low back and pelvic pain.

        Evidence and sources

        Every piece of advice in Nestling is linked to its source, and every claim carries a label showing how strong the evidence is. This page explains the labels and lists every source.

        How claims are graded

        Strong

        Consistent results from large, well-run studies, such as randomised trials (which assign people to groups by chance) or very large studies that follow people over time, or reviews that combine many studies. Unlikely to change with new research.

        Moderate

        Good-quality studies point the same way, but there are fewer of them, they observe families rather than test an intervention, or results vary. Could change with new research.

        Weak

        Small studies, or the agreed opinion of experts, without strong direct testing. Reasonable to try, but not proven.

        Not supported

        The claim has been studied, and the evidence does not back it.

        This four-level scheme is Nestling's own summary of how much trust a claim deserves. It is not the same as the formal grading systems used in guidelines, such as GRADE or the A to D levels of the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC).

        Reading the numbers in the source notes: an odds ratio or risk ratio compares how often something happened in two groups. 1 means no difference, below 1 means less likely, and above 1 means more likely (2 is roughly twice as likely). A plausible range shows how precise a result is: if it includes 1, the difference could be due to chance. An effect size describes how big a difference is: about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.

        Some statements carry a source tag but no label. These are official recommendations and facts, such as phone numbers, schedules, service details and safety rules, taken directly from Australian guidelines and services. Where a recommendation rests on research, the research is graded alongside it.

        How the sources were checked

        Research papers were checked in PubMed, the US National Library of Medicine's database: each PubMed ID (PMID) and DOI (a permanent link to a paper) was confirmed against the original record. Official advice was taken from the organisation's own website, and every link was accessed on 13 or 14 November 2026. Each source lists who was studied, so you can see when a finding is being applied to a different group, such as research done in other countries or in older children.

        Australian and Victorian guidance is used wherever it exists. International studies are included when they are the best available evidence.
